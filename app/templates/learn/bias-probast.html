What a bias is

A bias is a systematic error: it does not shrink with more patients, because it comes from how the patients were chosen, how the predictors or the outcome were measured, or how the analysis was done. Random error, by contrast, shrinks with more data and shows up in the confidence intervals.

For prediction models the standard tool is PROBAST: 4 domains, 20 signalling questions. For each domain we judge the risk of bias (low, high, unclear) and, in the first three, the concern about applicability: how well the study answers our clinical question. If even one domain is at high risk, the overall judgement is high.

The four domains

DomainTypical questions
1. ParticipantsWhat kind of study do the data come from? Who was included or excluded, and why?
2. PredictorsMeasured the same way for everyone? Without knowing the outcome? Available when the model is used?
3. OutcomeDefined in a standard way and the same for everyone? Assessed without knowing the predictors?
4. AnalysisEnough events? Continuous variables not cut into classes? Missing data handled well? Variable selection not based on univariable associations? Calibration and overfitting assessed?

A "high" judgement does not mean a study is useless: it means its numbers must not be used to decide about real patients. It remains useful to learn the method and to understand which data are needed.

Same tool, different biases

  • Obese: the main problem is the outcome (half of the Cormack grades with a video laryngoscope, which improves the view) and only 18 events.
  • Thyroid: the main problem is the participants. Excluding patients with an expected difficult intubation removes the most obvious cases: it is spectrum bias, and it makes the predictors look less useful than they are.
  • In both, the analysis has few events relative to the predictors: the only real remedy is more data, collected with the full airway examination, Cormack with a Macintosh blade and no exclusions up front.

Why not a "network" comparison across datasets

A network meta-analysis compares treatments that were never compared directly, going through a common arm (A versus B and B versus C say something about A versus C). There is nothing like that here: VitalDB, the obese-patient study and the thyroid study are different samples with different variables, not arms with a common comparator. They can only be compared descriptively, or by validating a model on the other dataset (transportability), which is what we did.
