What we want to predict

Before induction we want to know whether direct laryngoscopy will give a poor view of the glottis, so that a video laryngoscope, a bougie, a second operator or an awake strategy can be prepared in advance. The project's outcome is the Cormack-Lehane grade:

GradeWhat is seenIn the project
1most of the glottiseasy (0)
2only the posterior part of the glottis
3only the epiglottisdifficult (1)
4neither glottis nor epiglottis

The Cormack grade depends on the device: with a video laryngoscope the same person often has a better grade than with a Macintosh blade. This is why a Cormack grade must always be recorded together with the device, and two grades obtained with different devices are not the same outcome.

Bedside signs

No single sign predicts difficult laryngoscopy well. The best known is the Mallampati class (1–4: how much of the soft palate, uvula and pillars can be seen with the mouth open). Others: thyromental distance, mouth opening, neck movement, ability to protrude the jaw, weight, history of difficult intubation.

The El-Ganzouri index (1996)

El-Ganzouri and colleagues combined 7 of these signs into a score from 0 to 12, positive if ≥ 4. It was built on more than 10,000 patients, but with a different outcome from ours: grade IV only (about 1% of cases). Its weights are therefore not "tuned" to Cormack 3–4. The Assess page computes it from the raw items.

Why the population matters

The frequency of Cormack 3–4 changes a lot between populations: in VitalDB (general surgery, Korea) it was 2.5%; in the 500 thyroid-surgery patients from Padova 9.6%; in the 99 obese patients 18%. A model trained where the outcome is rare underestimates the risk where it is common: you can see it in the results pages (obese, thyroid).
