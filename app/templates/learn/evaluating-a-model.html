Why not accuracy

If 18% of patients have Cormack 3–4, a "model" that always answers easy has 82% accuracy and is useless. With uncommon outcomes, accuracy rewards ignoring the very cases we care about. Four other things are checked instead.

1. Discrimination: the AUC

Pick at random one patient with Cormack 3–4 and one with Cormack 1–2: the AUC is the probability that the model gives the first a higher risk. 0.5 = coin toss; 1 = perfect. In clinical practice 0.7 is modest, 0.8 good. The AUC does not say whether the numbers are right, only whether the order is right.

2. Calibration: are the numbers true?

If the model says 20% to a hundred patients, about twenty should have Cormack 3–4. It is checked with the predicted/observed chart and two numbers:

  • calibration-in-the-large: mean predicted risk versus observed frequency;
  • calibration slope: 1 is ideal; below 1 predictions are too extreme (typical of overfitting), above 1 too timid.

With a probability threshold, calibration is decisive: a model that ranks well but gets the numbers wrong puts the wrong people above or below the threshold.

3. The 15% threshold

"I prepare plan B if the risk is at least 15%" means accepting about 6 unnecessary preparations for every difficult laryngoscopy found (0.85 / 0.15 ≈ 5.7). It is a clinical choice, not a statistical one: preparing a video laryngoscope costs little, not having it costs a lot. At the threshold we report sensitivity, specificity, predictive values and the share of flagged patients.

4. Decision curve: is it really useful?

The net benefit at a threshold p counts true positives and subtracts false positives weighted by p/(1−p):

net benefit = TP/n − FP/n × p/(1 − p)

A model is useful only if it lies above the two trivial strategies: always prepare and never prepare (zero benefit).

How to estimate them honestly

Metrics computed on the same patients used to train the model are optimistic. Two ways to correct them:

  • cross-validation: train on 4/5 of the patients and evaluate on the fifth left out, in turn, repeating several times; every preprocessing step (imputation, standardization) is fitted on the training part only;
  • bootstrap optimism correction (Harrell): measure how much better a model trained on a bootstrap sample looks on that sample than on the original data, and subtract it. With few patients it is the recommended method.

With very few events even cross-validation has traps: in our obese-patient study the "null" model, which gives everyone the same risk, came out with AUC 0.31 instead of 0.50, because the frequency in the training folds changes slightly from fold to fold. This is why the AUC is computed within each fold.
