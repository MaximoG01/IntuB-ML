From data to the app, in five steps

  1. Data. 500 adults before thyroid surgery in Padova; 48 had Cormack 3–4 with a curved blade. Every sign was recorded before laryngoscopy.
  2. Prepare. Each sign is rescaled to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so that the weights can be compared and penalized fairly. No value is missing in these patients (for empty fields in the app see below).
  3. Fit. A logistic regression finds one weight per sign (how it learns), with a penalty that keeps the weights small. The strength of the penalty is chosen automatically (see below).
  4. Validate. The model is rebuilt 100 times, each time leaving out one fifth of the patients and scoring it on them (5-fold cross-validation × 20). A bootstrap estimates how optimistic the results are.
  5. Export. The final weights go into a small text file (model.json); the Assess page only multiplies and adds: the model is not retrained online.

What a logistic regression does

Each sign adds or subtracts a fixed amount to a score:

score = intercept + w₁·Mallampati III–IV + w₂·mouth < 4 cm + … + w₇·BMI

The logistic curve then turns the score into a probability. A patient with every sign at the average value sits near the prevalence (about 8–10%); each unfavourable sign pushes the point to the right along the curve. Above 15% the app suggests preparing plan B.

Why a penalty?

With only 48 events and 7 signs, an unpenalized logistic regression adapts to the noise of these particular patients: the weights come out too large and the predictions too extreme (overfitting). The L2 penalty (ridge) changes what is minimized:

prediction error + λ × (w₁² + w₂² + … + w₇²)

Large weights now "cost" something, so the model keeps them only if the data really support them. In scikit-learn the strength is written as C = 1/λ: small C = strong penalty. Ours was chosen by an inner cross-validation that minimizes the log-loss, a score that punishes badly calibrated probabilities, not only bad ranking.

What to notice

  • The AUC barely moves. Shrinking all the weights together keeps the patients in almost the same order, and the AUC only looks at the order.
  • The probabilities change a lot. With a very strong penalty every patient gets roughly the same risk, close to the prevalence: nobody crosses the 15% threshold, so a model with a "good" AUC becomes useless at the bedside. This is why calibration is checked separately (how to evaluate a model).
  • Without a penalty the AUC on the training patients stays above the AUC on unseen patients: that gap is the optimism that the validation removes.

What if a sign is not given?

The 500 Padova patients have no missing values, but in the Assess form a field can be left empty. Two options:

  • Fill the gap with the median of the training patients. For a yes/no sign the median is "no": an unknown Mallampati is treated as a reassuring one.
  • Leave the sign out: use a model fitted without it (a pattern submodel). With 7 signs there are 127 such models; they are all computed in advance.

We simulated both: the model is trained on complete data, then values are deleted from the patients it is tested on. O/E = observed ÷ expected events: above 1 means the risk is underestimated.
